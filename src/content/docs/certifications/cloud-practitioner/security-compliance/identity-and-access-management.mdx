## The idea in plain language

An office building gives each person an access card. A cleaner's card opens the storerooms. A finance
manager's opens the finance floor. Nobody needs a card that opens everything, and a visiting contractor
gets a temporary pass that stops working when the job ends.

**AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM)** does this for your AWS account. It controls who can sign in
and what they are allowed to do. There is no charge for IAM itself.

Two words matter here. **Authentication** proves who you are. **Authorization** decides what you may do.

Attaching a policy to a group gives those permissions to every user in it. This is easier to manage
than setting permissions person by person.

**Roles** provide temporary credentials. AWS recommends roles for workloads, and federation with
temporary credentials for people. A **cross-account IAM role** lets an identity in one AWS account work
in another account without a separate user there.

## The principle of least privilege

**Least privilege** means granting only the permissions needed to perform a task. If an account is
compromised, narrow permissions limit the damage.

In practice:

- Start with AWS managed policies, then move to customer managed policies that fit your needs.
- Review access regularly and remove unused users, roles, and credentials.
- Use last accessed information to find permissions nobody uses.

<ImportantConcept>
  Give people what they need for their job, and no more. "Administrator access for everyone" is
  convenient and dangerous.
</ImportantConcept>

## Federation and IAM Identity Center

**Federated identity** means people sign in with an identity they already have, such as a company
account, instead of a separate IAM user. They receive temporary credentials. AWS recommends federation
for human users.

**AWS IAM Identity Center** connects your workforce to multiple AWS accounts and applications. It can
use your existing identity provider, or you can create users in it directly. It is the successor to AWS
Single Sign-On.

<RealWorldExample title="Real-world example: a growing software company">
  A Stellenbosch software company has several AWS accounts. Staff sign in through IAM Identity
  Center with their company login. Developers receive permissions for the development account only.
  A monitoring tool uses a role, so no long-term password sits in its configuration. When someone
  leaves, disabling their company login removes access.
</RealWorldExample>

## What the exam expects

<ExamTip>
  **Only the permissions needed** means least privilege. **Same permissions for a team** points to
  groups. **Temporary access, or access for an application** points to roles. **Sign in with
  existing company accounts across AWS accounts** points to IAM Identity Center.
</ExamTip>

## Common misconceptions

- **"A role belongs to one person."** A role is not tied to a specific person. It is assumed when
  needed.
- **"Groups can sign in."** A group is a collection of users. It is not an identity you sign in with.
- **"Everyone needs an IAM user."** AWS recommends federation with temporary credentials for people.

## Try it yourself (optional)

You need no AWS account for this, and it costs nothing. Pick three jobs at a company, such as accountant,
developer, and auditor. For each, write the smallest set of permissions the person needs. Then decide
whether a group or a role fits best.

## Summary

<LessonSummary>

- IAM controls who can sign in and what they can do.
- Users are identities, groups collect users, roles give temporary access, and policies list
  permissions.
- Policies can be AWS managed, customer managed, or inline.
- Least privilege grants only the permissions a task needs.
- IAM Identity Center and federation let people use existing identities.

</LessonSummary>
