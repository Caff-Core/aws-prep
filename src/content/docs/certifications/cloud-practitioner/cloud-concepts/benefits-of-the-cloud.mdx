## The idea in plain language

Imagine you need electricity at home. You could buy a generator, store fuel, and repair it yourself.
Most people plug into the grid instead. They use what they need and pay for what they used.

Cloud computing applies that idea to IT. **Cloud computing** is the on-demand delivery of IT resources
over the internet, with pay-as-you-go pricing. Instead of buying and maintaining your own servers, you
use computing power, storage, and databases from a provider such as AWS when you need them.

The alternative is **on-premises** computing, where an organisation owns and runs its own equipment,
usually in its own building or a rented data center.

## The six advantages of cloud computing

AWS describes six advantages. The names are worth learning, because exam questions often describe a
situation and expect you to recognise which one it shows.

### Trade fixed expense for variable expense

Building a data center means paying a large amount before you know how much you will use. With the
cloud, you pay only for what you consume. A **fixed expense** stays the same however much you use. A
**variable expense** rises and falls with use.

### Benefit from massive economies of scale

AWS combines the usage of a very large number of customers. That scale lets it offer lower
pay-as-you-go prices than most organisations could achieve alone.

### Stop guessing capacity

**Capacity** is how much work your systems can handle. On-premises, you must predict it in advance.
Guess too high and expensive equipment sits idle. Guess too low and your systems struggle. In the
cloud, you can scale up or down as demand changes.

### Increase speed and agility

New IT resources are available in minutes rather than weeks. This lowers the cost and time of trying
out new ideas, so teams can experiment more often.

### Stop spending money running and maintaining data centers

Time spent racking and powering servers is time not spent on your customers. The cloud lets
organisations focus on their own business.

### Go global in minutes

You can deploy an application in multiple AWS Regions around the world, which puts it closer to your
users and lowers latency. The [AWS global infrastructure](/certifications/cloud-practitioner/technology-services/global-infrastructure/)
lesson explains how this works.

## Three words the exam separates

Three terms sound similar but answer different questions.

### Elasticity

**Elasticity** is the ability to match resources to demand as it changes. You provision what you need
now, then scale up or down, rather than paying for your busiest day all year.

### High availability

**Availability** is the percentage of time a workload is available for use. **High availability**
means designing a system so that it stays usable even when part of it fails, for example by running
across multiple Availability Zones.

### Agility

**Agility** is how quickly an organisation can act. Easy access to a wide range of technology means
teams can build and test ideas sooner, and drop the ones that do not work at little cost.

<ImportantConcept>
  Elasticity handles changing **demand**. High availability handles **failure**. Agility is about
  how fast your **organisation** can move.
</ImportantConcept>

## Elasticity in practice

<ArchitectureDiagram spec={elasticWebApp} />

**Amazon EC2 Auto Scaling** keeps the right number of servers running for the current load. You set a
minimum and a maximum, and it launches or terminates servers as demand rises and falls. Amazon Elastic
Compute Cloud (Amazon EC2) is the AWS service that provides these virtual servers.

<RealWorldExample title="Real-world example: selling concert tickets">
  A Cape Town ticketing company sees quiet traffic most days, then a huge rush when a popular show
  goes on sale. On-premises, it would need enough servers for that rush, and they would sit idle the
  rest of the year. On AWS, it runs a small number of servers normally and lets Auto Scaling add
  more during the rush. It pays for the extra capacity only while it is running.
</RealWorldExample>

<CostWarning>
  Pay-as-you-go means resources you leave running keep costing money, even if nobody uses them. Auto
  Scaling has no extra charge of its own, but you pay for the resources it launches.
</CostWarning>

## What the exam expects

<ExamTip>
  Look for the clue in the scenario. **Unpredictable traffic** points to elasticity or stop guessing
  capacity. **No upfront investment** points to trading fixed expense for variable expense. **Weeks
  reduced to minutes** points to speed and agility. **Surviving a failure** points to high
  availability, not elasticity.
</ExamTip>

## Common misconceptions

- **"The cloud is always cheaper."** You pay for what you use, so costs depend on how you use it.
  Resources left running are still billed.
- **"Elasticity and high availability are the same."** A system can scale perfectly and still go down
  if everything runs in one place. They solve different problems.
- **"Moving to the cloud means AWS handles everything."** AWS runs the data centers, but customers
  still have responsibilities. The
  [shared responsibility model](/certifications/cloud-practitioner/security-compliance/shared-responsibility-model/)
  lesson covers this.

## Try it yourself (optional)

You need no AWS account for this, and it costs nothing. Think of a business you know, such as a shop or
a clinic. On paper, write down which of the six advantages would matter most to it, and why. Then
decide whether its demand is steady or changes sharply. That tells you how much elasticity would help.
