## The idea in plain language

When you use a website, your request travels to a computer somewhere in the world. If that computer
is far away, the site feels slow. If it is the only one and it fails, the site disappears.

AWS addresses both problems by building its infrastructure in layers. Think of a national retail
chain. It has a head office in each country, several separate warehouses around each major city, and
small pick-up points close to customers. If one warehouse floods, the others keep shipping. The
pick-up points get popular items into customers' hands quickly.

AWS has three matching layers: **Regions**, **Availability Zones**, and **edge locations**.

## The three layers

### Regions

A **Region** is a physical location in the world where AWS clusters data centers. Each Region has its
own name and code. For example, Africa (Cape Town) has the code `af-south-1`.

You choose which Regions to use. When you create most resources, you create them in a Region you have
selected.

### Availability Zones

An **Availability Zone**, often shortened to AZ, is one or more discrete data centers with redundant
power, networking, and connectivity in a Region. Each Region has at least three Availability Zones.

Availability Zones in a Region are:

- **Physically separated** by a meaningful distance, and all within 100 km (60 miles) of each other.
- **Independent.** Common points of failure, such as generators and cooling equipment, are not shared
  between them.
- **Connected** with high-bandwidth, low-latency networking over redundant, dedicated metro fiber.

<ImportantConcept>
  Distance protects against shared disasters such as floods or power failures. Fast links let
  Availability Zones work together as if they were close. You need both for high availability.
</ImportantConcept>

### Edge locations

An **edge location** is a site in a worldwide network that Amazon CloudFront uses to deliver content.
CloudFront is the AWS content delivery service.

When a user requests content, CloudFront routes the request to the edge location with the lowest
latency, which means the shortest delay. Copies of files can be held there, in a cache, so they do not
have to travel from their origin each time.

**High availability** means a system keeps working even when part of it fails. In this design, a
failure in Availability Zone A does not have to stop the website, because servers in Availability Zone
B can keep serving users. A single Availability Zone would be a single point of failure.

<RealWorldExample title="Real-world example: an online retailer">
  A South African online shop runs its website in the Africa (Cape Town) Region, on servers in two
  Availability Zones behind a load balancer. Product images are delivered through CloudFront, so
  shoppers receive cached copies from a nearby edge location. During a sale, a power fault hits one
  Availability Zone. Customers can keep shopping, because the load balancer sends requests to the
  healthy servers.
</RealWorldExample>

## Choosing where to run

AWS advises selecting a Region that has the services and features you need. A Region close to most of
your users lowers network latency. Location can also matter for regulatory, compliance, and legal
requirements.

Sometimes one Region is not enough. The exam guide lists these reasons to use multiple Regions:

- **Disaster recovery** and **business continuity**: somewhere to recover if a whole Region is affected.
- **Low latency for end users**: serving customers on different continents from nearby Regions.
- **Data sovereignty**: keeping data in a particular country to meet legal requirements.

<CostWarning>
  Running in several Availability Zones or Regions means running more resources, and that can
  increase your bill. Check the pricing of each service for the Regions you use.
</CostWarning>

## What the exam expects

<ExamTip>
  Match the need to the layer. **Survive a data center failure** points to multiple Availability
  Zones. **Survive a Regional disaster**, or keep data in a country, points to Regions. **Deliver
  content faster to users worldwide** points to edge locations.
</ExamTip>

Avoid memorising how many Regions AWS has. The number changes, and the concepts are what matter.

## Common misconceptions

- **"An Availability Zone is one building."** It can be one or more discrete data centers.
- **"Edge locations run my whole application."** In this lesson's design, they deliver cached content
  close to users. The servers stay in the Region.
- **"One Availability Zone is highly available."** It is a single point of failure for your
  application. High availability comes from using more than one.

## Summary

<LessonSummary>

- A Region is a physical location containing at least three Availability Zones. - Availability
  Zones are separate, independent, and linked by fast networking. - Using multiple Availability
  Zones provides high availability. - Multiple Regions support disaster recovery, low latency, and
  data sovereignty. - Edge locations let CloudFront deliver content close to users with lower
  latency.

</LessonSummary>
